After finishing my studies, I wanted to look back on one of the biggest discoveries, and proudest achievements, of my degree: hackathons.

Little known to the general public, the hackathon is a classic of the dev/tech world.
In a few words, it's an event where people come together for a short period of time to work on building a project together.

Through this mini-series, I want to share the love I've grown for these events over the years: my experience, my successes, my failures and what I learned. I'm writing for:
- **students who have never done one, beginners or experienced.** A hackathon will boost your studies, in terms of knowledge, but also open-mindedness, the people you meet and the opportunities you get.
- **those who think hackathons are only for devs.** Every field stands to gain from bringing people together and catalysing ideas as intensely as a hackathon does.
- **those who think hackathons are for students or job seekers.** Spoiler: I'm going to keep doing them even though I've finished my studies!
- **more experienced people who could organise, mentor or fund a hackathon.** Got a project to build or to promote? Want to pass on your expertise? A hackathon is a playground.
- **everyone else,** just curious to read about my adventures! (mum, dad and grandma, basically)

Without further ado, a bit of history.
## There's code, and then there's code

When you start at École 42, you only learn to code in C. You get the basics of computing and programming by writing low-level programs. You discover fundamental concepts like graphics, 3D, memory, networking... In other words, nothing to do with the web!

And yet my first hackathon was about building a website. HTML, CSS and JavaScript, neatly packaged to showcase a festival in Lyon (Les Nuits de Fourvière). This was in partnership with marketing students who had made the mockups.

All in 2 weeks. I can hear you shouting at your screen: "2 weeks for a site that's already designed? Give Claude a Figma link and it's done in 30 minutes!"

And I'd answer that this was early 2023, and Claude 2 hadn't yet been released to the world. We had ChatGPT on the web, but no AI was writing code for me.

So you had to know what you were doing (sounds crazy, right?).

## Carelessness as a trigger

Anyway, I went out of pure curiosity, because I wanted to work with people who weren't devs. Without really knowing what I was getting into. Without knowing that my two teammates from school would drop out after 2 days, because it wasn't what they expected. Without knowing that I'd end up accountable to 6 marketing students. Without knowing that I had none of the skills needed for the challenge.

If I'd known, I probably wouldn't have done it. Like many other students, I would have told myself I didn't have the necessary knowledge. That I'd never built a website. That I couldn't produce anything serious in the time available.

And that would have been my worst mistake. Because 3 years and 7 hackathons later, I can tell you that a hackathon is never easy. It's never something you've done before. Nor a perfectly familiar setting. It's a challenge. A leap into the unknown. A countless number of questions: what will our project be? In what context? What's at stake? Who will I meet? Will I make it? Will it be worth it?

Don't try to answer them: they'll stay unanswered until you're there!

## Hackathon misconceptions

That said, I want to break a few preconceptions right away. A hackathon does not mean:

- **a coding competition.** Nobody has ever looked at our code.
- **mastering the subject before you arrive.** You learn on the job, with others. You make do with what you've got. The goal isn't necessarily to win, since you may be up against far more experienced people, but you gain an enormous amount for yourself.
- **being very technical.** Quite the opposite: generally, the less you focus on the technical side, the better you perform.
- **no sleep and no shower for 24 hours.** Well, sometimes yes. But not always!
- **free labour.** A hackathon is an exchange. On one side, participants work on real, concrete cases, gain experience and meet people. On the other, organisers often run hackathons to promote their product, their hiring opportunities or their image. Give and take.

At my first hackathon, my two teammates from 42 dropped out after 2 days. Same again at the second one: you didn't really need to code, it was mostly project management.
We come out of a curriculum where value is measured in validated projects and lines written, and we land somewhere nobody will look at the code. Not everyone likes that!

So I'll say it plainly: if you're coming to code, you may leave disappointed.

## 7 experiences

In the next episodes, I'll take these misconceptions apart and walk you through my 5 main lessons. They're based on 7 hackathons, whose context I'm laying out here once and for all, so I don't have to repeat it in every episode.

1. **Nuits de Fourvière** — reproduce the mockup of a music festival's website. 🕒 10 days · 📍 Lyon · 2023. With marketing students.
2. **Nuit de l'Innovation à Impact Solidaire (NIIS)** — design a community garden project in Lyon. 🕒 24h · 📍 Lyon · 2024. With students from all kinds of schools (business, impact project management, marketing, code...). 🏆 Award winner.
3. **Raycast** — build an AI extension for the Raycast app. 🕒 6h · 📍 London · 2025. With more experienced developers from all over Europe.
4. **TRACS** — solve challenges to score points and finish at the top of the leaderboard. 🕒 10h · 📍 Paris · 2025. With developer students, focused on cybersecurity.
5. **Commissariat au Numérique de Défense (CND)** — build log-analysis software against cyberattacks. 🕒 3 days · 📍 Paris · 2025. Among developers. 🏆 Award winner.
6. **Unboxed AI** — build AI software to analyse knee X-rays. 🕒 48h · 📍 Lyon · 2026. With dev, healthcare and business profiles. 🏆 Award winner.
7. **La Suite** — build features for the sovereign alternative to Office 365. 🕒 5 days · 📍 Île d'Oléron · 2026. With 42 developers from every French campus. 🏆 Award winner.

## The common ingredients

Seven formats, seven subjects, seven settings. And yet, the same three ingredients almost always come up:

- **a real problem**, brought by someone whose job it is.
- **very different people**, in the same room.
- **a short deadline**, which forces you to make decisions.

## So, Fourvière?

**Did I manage to deliver a website for Les Nuits de Fourvière?**
Yes.

**Was it nice-looking and functional?**
Not really.

**Was I ashamed of knowing nothing about it?**
Yes. The first two days were very hard. It got better after that.

**Did I regret the experience?**
At times, in the thick of it, yes. Looking back, not in the slightest.
